Generative AI refers to a class of artificial intelligence systems that create new content, such as text, images, audio, video, and code, rather than only classifying or predicting from existing data. Traditional machine learning models are usually trained for a narrow task, such as detecting spam or forecasting sales. Generative models instead learn the statistical structure of very large datasets and use it to produce novel outputs that resemble the data they were trained on. This shift from narrow, task-specific models to flexible, general-purpose systems is what has made generative AI useful across so many domains, including customer support, software development, education, and content creation.

At the center of modern generative AI are foundation models. A foundation model is a large neural network, most often based on the transformer architecture, that is pretrained on a broad corpus of data at massive scale and can then be adapted to many downstream tasks. Pretraining is typically self-supervised, meaning the model learns from raw data without human-written labels. For language models, the training objective is usually to predict the next token in a sequence. After pretraining, a foundation model can be used directly through prompting, specialized through fine-tuning on a smaller dataset, or aligned with human preferences through techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback. Because one model can serve many purposes, organizations can build applications on top of it without training a new model for every task.

A Large Language Model, or LLM, is a foundation model specialized for understanding and generating human language. LLMs contain billions of parameters, which are the numerical weights adjusted during training. They do not read text as words or characters directly. Instead, they rely on tokenization, the process of splitting text into smaller units called tokens. A token can be a whole word, a part of a word, a single character, or a piece of punctuation. Most modern tokenizers use subword algorithms such as Byte Pair Encoding, which starts with individual characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pairs until a vocabulary of a fixed size is built. Common words often become a single token, while rare or long words are split into several pieces. This approach lets the model handle misspellings, new words, and many languages without needing an unmanageable vocabulary.

Once text is tokenized, each token is mapped to an integer ID, and each ID is mapped to a vector of numbers through an embedding layer. The transformer then processes these vectors using self-attention, a mechanism that lets every token weigh its relationship to every other token in the sequence. This is how the model captures context, such as understanding that the word bank means something different near the word river than near the word loan. During generation, the model produces a probability distribution over its entire vocabulary for the next token, selects one according to a decoding strategy, appends it to the sequence, and repeats the process. Parameters such as temperature and top-p control how random this selection is. A low temperature makes the output more deterministic and focused, while a higher temperature makes it more varied and creative. Token counts also matter practically, because API pricing, speed, and limits are all measured in tokens rather than words.

Prompt engineering is the practice of designing and refining the input given to an LLM so that it produces useful, accurate, and well-formatted output. Because the model's behavior is steered almost entirely by the text it receives, small changes in wording, structure, or examples can lead to very different results. Core techniques include zero-shot prompting, where the model is given only an instruction, and few-shot prompting, where a handful of input and output examples are included to demonstrate the desired pattern. Chain-of-thought prompting encourages the model to reason step by step before answering, which often improves performance on logic, math, and multi-step problems. Good prompts are specific about the task, the audience, the tone, and the output format, and they provide any relevant context the model would otherwise have to guess.

In chat applications, prompts are organized into messages that carry roles. The system role holds high-level instructions that define the assistant's persona, rules, tone, and constraints, for example telling the model to act as a concise technical tutor and to refuse requests outside a defined scope. The user role contains the messages from the person using the application, and the assistant role contains the model's previous replies. LLMs are stateless, which means they do not remember earlier turns on their own. To create the experience of a continuous conversation, the application must resend the accumulated message history with every new request. The model reads the entire history as one long input and generates the next assistant message in response.

This is where the context window becomes important. The context window is the maximum number of tokens a model can process in a single request, and it must hold the system message, the conversation history, any retrieved documents, and the space reserved for the model's reply. When a conversation grows beyond this limit, the application must decide what to drop or compress. Common strategies include truncating the oldest messages, keeping a sliding window of recent turns, or summarizing earlier parts of the conversation into a shorter message. Even within the limit, very long contexts can increase cost and latency, and models may pay less attention to information buried in the middle of a long input. Careful context management is therefore a core part of building reliable chat applications.

Text embeddings are a way of representing the meaning of text as a list of numbers, called a vector. An embedding model takes a word, sentence, paragraph, or document and outputs a vector with a fixed number of dimensions, often between several hundred and a few thousand. The model is trained so that pieces of text with similar meaning produce vectors that sit close together in this high-dimensional space, while unrelated text produces vectors that are far apart. As a result, a sentence about adopting a puppy and a sentence about getting a new dog will have nearby embeddings even though they share few exact words. Individual dimensions are not human-interpretable, but together they encode semantic relationships, topics, and context in a form that computers can compare mathematically.

To measure how close two embeddings are, systems use a similarity or distance metric. The most widely used is cosine similarity, which computes the cosine of the angle between two vectors. It is calculated as the dot product of the two vectors divided by the product of their magnitudes. The result ranges from negative one to one. A value near one means the vectors point in nearly the same direction, indicating very similar meaning. A value near zero means the vectors are roughly orthogonal and unrelated, and a negative value indicates opposing directions. Cosine similarity focuses on direction rather than length, which makes it robust when texts differ in size. Other common metrics include Euclidean distance, which measures straight-line distance between points, and the dot product, which is equivalent to cosine similarity when the vectors are normalized to unit length. Cosine distance is simply one minus cosine similarity, so smaller distances mean more similar content.

Embeddings make semantic search possible. In traditional keyword search, a query matches a document only if it shares the same terms, so a search for car repair might miss a document about automobile maintenance. In semantic search, both the query and each document are converted into embeddings, and results are ranked by their similarity to the query vector. This allows the system to retrieve relevant content based on meaning, handle synonyms and paraphrases, and support natural-language questions. Embeddings are also used for recommendations, clustering, duplicate detection, classification, and anomaly detection.

Computing similarity against every stored vector works for a few thousand documents, but it becomes too slow when a corpus contains millions or billions of embeddings. An exact search, often called brute force or k-nearest neighbors search, compares the query against every vector and scales linearly with the size of the dataset. High-dimensional spaces make this even more expensive, and traditional relational databases are not designed to index or query vectors efficiently. This is why vector databases are necessary. A vector database is a data store built specifically to store embeddings together with their metadata and to retrieve the most similar vectors quickly.

Vector databases achieve speed by using approximate nearest neighbor, or ANN, algorithms. Instead of guaranteeing the mathematically exact closest vectors, ANN methods trade a small amount of accuracy for large gains in speed and memory efficiency. Popular approaches include Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs, which build a layered graph of vectors so that a search can hop quickly from distant regions toward the neighborhood of the query. Another approach is the inverted file index, which clusters vectors and searches only the most relevant clusters. Product quantization compresses vectors into compact codes to reduce memory usage. In practice, ANN search can return highly accurate results in milliseconds even over enormous collections. Vector databases also support metadata filtering, so a query can be limited to documents from a certain source, date, or user, and they handle inserting, updating, and deleting vectors as data changes. Examples include Pinecone, Weaviate, Milvus, Qdrant, Chroma, and vector search features built into databases such as PostgreSQL with pgvector, Azure AI Search, and Elasticsearch.

Despite their fluency, LLMs have well-known limitations. Their knowledge is frozen at the time of training, so they cannot know about recent events or private company data. They can also hallucinate, meaning they generate statements that sound confident and plausible but are incorrect or entirely fabricated. This happens because the model is optimized to produce likely text, not to verify facts. For applications where accuracy matters, such as customer support, legal research, or internal knowledge assistants, relying only on the model's internal memory is risky.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation, commonly called RAG, addresses these problems by combining a retrieval system with a generative model. In a typical RAG pipeline, documents are first prepared in an ingestion phase. They are split into smaller chunks, often a few hundred tokens each with some overlap so that ideas are not cut off mid-thought. Each chunk is converted into an embedding and stored in a vector database along with its source and other metadata. Chunk size is an important design choice, because chunks that are too large dilute relevance and waste context space, while chunks that are too small may lose the surrounding meaning.

At query time, the system embeds the user's question using the same embedding model, searches the vector database for the most similar chunks, and retrieves the top results. These retrieved passages are then inserted into the prompt as context, usually alongside an instruction telling the model to answer using only the provided information and to say that it does not know if the answer is not present. The LLM then generates a response grounded in this supplied text. Because the answer is based on retrieved evidence rather than on the model's memory alone, the likelihood of hallucination drops significantly, and the system can point to the source documents it used, which makes the answer easier to verify and trust.

RAG offers several practical advantages over retraining or fine-tuning a model. Knowledge can be updated simply by adding or replacing documents in the vector database, with no need to retrain anything. Private or proprietary data stays in a controlled store and is only supplied to the model when relevant. Access controls can be applied at the retrieval layer so users only see information they are permitted to access. It is also generally cheaper and faster to maintain than repeated fine-tuning. However, RAG quality depends heavily on retrieval quality. If the retriever returns irrelevant or incomplete chunks, the model may produce a poor answer or ignore the context. Teams often improve results through better chunking strategies, hybrid search that combines keyword and vector search, re-ranking models that reorder retrieved results, query rewriting, and evaluation of both retrieval accuracy and answer faithfulness.

Taken together, these components form a coherent stack. Tokenization and foundation models provide the language capability, prompt engineering and system roles shape how that capability behaves in chat applications, and the context window defines how much information the model can consider at once. Embeddings translate meaning into vectors, cosine similarity measures how related those vectors are, and vector databases make that comparison fast at scale. RAG ties everything together by retrieving relevant knowledge and feeding it to the model at the moment of generation, producing responses that are fluent, current, and grounded in real-world data.
